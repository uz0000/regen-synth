A blind spot in fake data

Fake data can pass every test and still give you the wrong answer.

When real data is too sensitive to share, people replace it with synthetic data — made-up records meant to stand in for the real ones. The usual checks ask whether it looks real and whether a prediction tool still works on it. They never ask the thing that matters most: if you analyze the fake data to understand why something happens, do you reach the same conclusion the real data would? Often you don't — and nothing warns you. This page is about a tool that catches that, and a way to make fake data that keeps the conclusion intact.

The problem

There are three questions you can ask of fake data. The usual checks only ask two.

Before trusting fake data, how do you know it's any good? In practice people check two things — and skip the one that decisions actually rest on. That third question is the dangerous one: the data can look perfect and still lead you to the wrong conclusion, with no warning.

Usually checked

Does it look real?

Does the fake data have the same range and mix as the real data — the same spread of ages, balances, and so on?

Usually checked

Can you predict with it?

If you train a prediction tool on the fake data, does it still make good predictions on real data?

the blind spot Almost never checked

Do your conclusions hold?

If you analyze the fake data to learn how much one thing affects another, do you get the same answer the real data would? This is the one that breaks.

How much warning do the usual checks give? Measured, not assumed: the Gaussian copula matches every column almost exactly (largest gap 0.010) and reproduces the correlations (largest shift 0.078), so how real the data looks tells you nothing here. Some of the others are caught, but by margins of hundredths, and only at strict cut-offs the field does not agree on. The full measurement is in FINDINGS.md, section 3.
Why it happens

Each column comes out right. The connections between them don't.

Here's the trap. The fake data can copy the shape of every column perfectly — the right spread of late payments, the right mix of balances. That's why it looks real. What it gets wrong is how those columns connect. Picture a crowd where you keep exactly the right number of tall people and the right number of heavy people, but you scramble who is who: each list looks perfect, yet “taller people tend to be heavier” falls apart. That kind of connection is exactly what an analysis leans on — and it's what the fake data quietly breaks. The honest limit — and the fix

Keeping the answer and protecting privacy fight each other.

To protect privacy you have to push the fake records away from the real ones. Do that the obvious way — add noise — and you wreck the answer before you've gained any real privacy. Build the fake records from a good model of the real data instead, and you can keep the answer with genuinely new rows — but only up to a point. You can steer this tradeoff; you can't escape it. How it works

Two parts: a check, and a better way to make the data.

Part one — the check

Did your conclusion survive?

You give it your real data, the fake data, and the question you care about. It runs your analysis on both, compares the answers, and hands back a certificate: did the conclusion hold?

  • It works on fake data from any source — it checks the answer, not who made it.
  • It tells you exactly which conclusions held and which didn't — not just a single yes or no.
  • Anyone can re-run the check from the fake data and the certificate alone. They never need the real records.
Part two — the fix

Fake data that keeps the answer

The usual methods copy real records and blur them, which breaks the connections between columns. This does the opposite: it learns the real patterns first, then builds brand-new records from them.

  • Because it captures how the columns connect — not just each one alone — the conclusion survives.
  • No record is copied from a real person, and no answer is forced in — every value comes from the learned patterns.
  • It's the one method in the table above that passes the check.
Why it holds up

Four properties that make the certificate worth trusting.

A

It checks fake data from any source — a popular tool, a neural network, a vendor's product, or this one. What matters is the answer, not where the data came from.

B

Not a blanket pass or fail. It names which conclusions held and which didn't, so you know exactly what the data can be trusted for.

The certificate travels with the data. Someone else can re-run it and get the same verdict — without ever seeing your real records.

D

Same result every time

Run it again, get the same certificate. Every number comes from a fixed calculation — nothing is a one-off guess from an AI.

Scope & honesty

What it's good for — and what it isn't.

Good for

  • Checking the kind of conclusions you'd draw from a standard analysis
  • Fake data from any source
  • A certificate anyone can re-check
  • Measuring the privacy cost in plain terms, and stating it

Not for

  • One simple table only — not data over time, linked tables, text, or images
  • You have to say up front which analysis matters — it can't cover ones you didn't name
  • Number columns today; categories come next
  • It isn't a formal mathematical privacy guarantee

The short version. Fake data can quietly break the conclusions you'd draw from it. This is a tool that checks whether it did — on fake data from anywhere — and a way to make fake data that keeps them, with the privacy cost measured honestly.
